MaxCut

Note

A Jupyter notebook related to this example is available in our examples folder.

The cost function for the MaxCut problem as defined in the original QAOA paper is

\[\begin{align*} C(\sigma) = \frac 12 \sum_{(i, j) \in E(G)} (1 - \sigma_i \sigma_j), \end{align*}\]

where $E(G)$ is the set of edges of the graph $G$. Spin $\sigma_i = \pm 1$ says which of the two groups node $i$ joins, and $C$ counts the edges between the groups. The goal is to maximize $C$.

As an example, we take the Petersen graph from Graphs.jl. It has 10 nodes and 15 edges, and its largest cut has 12 edges. We can set this model up as follows:

using QAOA, Graphs, Random
graph = smallgraph(:petersen)
N = nv(graph)

h = zeros(N)
J = zeros(N, N)
for edge in edges(graph)
    J[src(edge), dst(edge)] = J[dst(edge), src(edge)] = -1/2
end

We have two options to get the corresponding IsingProblem from QAOA.jl. We can pass h and the coupling matrix J directly, together with the constant $|E(G)|/2$ of $C$:

max_cut_problem = IsingProblem(h, J; offset = ne(graph) / 2, sense = :max)
IsingProblem{Float64}: 10 spins, sense = :max

Note that QAOA.jl minimizes the cost function by default, so for MaxCut we pass sense = :max. Alternatively, we can use a predefined wrapper function that constructs J from the graph and directly returns an IsingProblem:

max_cut_problem = max_cut(graph)
IsingProblem{Float64}: 10 spins, sense = :max

Without Graphs.jl, the wrapper takes the number of nodes and a list of edges instead, as in max_cut(10, [(1, 2), (1, 5), …]).

To see how often the QAOA finds a largest cut, we compute the cut of every bitstring. In bitstring b, spin $\sigma_i = +1$ where bit i - 1 of the integer b is 1:

spins(b) = [2 * ((b >> (i - 1)) & 1) - 1 for i in 1:N]
cut_sizes = [objective(max_cut_problem, spins(b)) for b in 0:2^N-1]
maximum(cut_sizes)
12.0

Annealing

We are going to evaluate many angles for the same problem, so we first build a Workspace. It holds the cost of every bitstring and the quantum state:

ws = Workspace(max_cut_problem)
Workspace for IsingProblem{Float64}: 10 spins, sense = :max: 10 qubits, folded to 9 by symmetry, objective as a table

Flipping all spins leaves every cut unchanged, since the two groups only swap names. The workspace uses this symmetry and stores only half of the quantum state.

Simulating quantum annealing with QAOA.jl is very simple. For a linear annealing schedule with p layers, all we need is linear_schedule. More layers make the annealing slower and the result better:

for p in (10, 30, 100)
    schedule = linear_schedule(p)
    P = probabilities(ws, schedule)
    println("p = $p: average cut ", round(expectation(ws, schedule); digits = 3),
            ", probability of a largest cut ", round(sum(P[cut_sizes .== 12]); digits = 3))
end
p = 10: average cut 11.454, probability of a largest cut 0.549
p = 30: average cut 11.96, probability of a largest cut 0.962
p = 100: average cut 11.999, probability of a largest cut 0.999

Optimized Angles

Given ws, we can also optimize the angles of p layers with optimize_schedule, which uses exact gradients. It adds one layer at a time: the optimum for p - 1 layers, interpolated to p layers, is where the search for p layers starts.

for p in 1:5
    tuned, value = optimize_schedule(ws, p)
    P = probabilities(ws, tuned)
    println("p = $p: average cut ", round(value; digits = 3),
            ", probability of a largest cut ", round(sum(P[cut_sizes .== 12]); digits = 3))
end
p = 1: average cut 10.387, probability of a largest cut 0.168
p = 2: average cut 11.105, probability of a largest cut 0.449
p = 3: average cut 11.724, probability of a largest cut 0.844
p = 4: average cut 11.937, probability of a largest cut 0.977
p = 5: average cut 11.969, probability of a largest cut 0.987

Four optimized layers find a largest cut about as often as 30 layers of annealing. The optimized angles resemble an annealing schedule: $\beta$ falls from layer to layer, and $\gamma$ mostly rises.

tuned, _ = optimize_schedule(ws, 4)
tuned
Schedule{Float64} with 4 layers: β = [0.5225372557960075, 0.3202627899259832, 0.2521217294636332, 0.15134678796003714], γ = [0.33300575991572173, 0.6869261625644589, 0.9970399601592308, 0.9247515751166967]

Finally, solve measures the final state 1000 times and returns the best cut it saw:

result = solve(ws, tuned; rng = Xoshiro(1))
Result{Float64}: 10 variables, objective = 12.0, expectation = 11.936517001296131
